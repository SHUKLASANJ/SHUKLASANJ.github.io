About me

I’m a physicist interested in how fluids move, interact, and break apart, from quantised vortices in superfluid helium to the interiors of neutron stars and turbulent multiphase flows.

I am currently a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Applied Physics and Science Education at Eindhoven University of Technology, where I work with Federico Toschi. My current research focuses on the dynamics of complex fluids, particularly droplet breakup, emulsions, and turbulence in fluids with odd viscosity.

Before joining TU Eindhoven, I completed my PhD in Physics at the Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore, in 2024. My doctoral research explored quantum turbulence in superfluid helium, the dynamics of neutron-star superfluids and pulsar glitches, and self-gravitating condensates as models of dark matter.

Across these different systems, I am interested in understanding how vortices, instabilities, and nonlinear interactions shape the dynamics and statistical properties of fluids. My research combines theoretical modelling, numerical simulations, and statistical physics, with an emphasis on large-scale computational approaches.

Research

Vortices, turbulence, and nonlinear dynamics in quantum and classical fluids

My research explores the dynamics of complex fluid systems across a wide range of physical scales, from quantum fluids at ultralow temperatures to classical multiphase flows and astrophysical condensates.

I am particularly interested in how vortices, instabilities, and nonlinear interactions govern the evolution and statistical properties of these systems. Using theoretical models, numerical simulations, and statistical analysis, I investigate the physical mechanisms underlying turbulent and nonequilibrium phenomena.

Multiphase flows and complex fluid turbulence

My current research at Eindhoven University of Technology focuses on the dynamics of complex fluids and multiphase turbulent flows. These systems involve interacting fluid interfaces, droplets, particles, and nonlinear flow structures, giving rise to a wide range of dynamical phenomena.

Working with Federico Toschi, I investigate problems such as liquid ligament breakup in the presence of solid particles, droplet dynamics, emulsions in turbulent flows, and the influence of odd viscosity on turbulent transport and energy transfer.

A central goal is to understand how interfacial dynamics, particle–fluid interactions, and non-dissipative transport mechanisms modify the behaviour of complex fluid systems.

Quantum turbulence and superfluid dynamics

At temperatures below approximately 2 K, liquid helium enters the superfluid state, where its rotation is carried by quantised vortices. The resulting quantum turbulence exhibits a rich interplay between discrete vortex structures and large-scale turbulent motion.

My research investigates the statistical properties and dynamics of quantum turbulence using the two-fluid Hall–Vinen–Bekharevich–Khalatnikov (HVBK) model and the Gross–Pitaevskii equation. I have studied particle transport in superfluid coflow and counterflow, the statistical properties of turbulent vortex tangles, and the capture and release of individual quantum vortices by vibrating nanowires.

Research topics
  • Statistical properties of quantum turbulence
  • Particle transport in superfluid turbulence
  • Quantised vortex dynamics and trapping
  • Numerical modelling of superfluid systems

Astrophysical superfluids: neutron stars and magnetars

Neutron stars provide a fascinating setting in which quantum fluids and superconducting matter operate under extreme conditions. Their interiors are believed to contain neutron superfluids and proton superconductors, where the interactions between quantised vortices and magnetic flux tubes can influence stellar rotation and magnetic-field evolution.

My research investigates the dynamics of these astrophysical superfluids, with particular emphasis on pulsar glitches and magnetar evolution. Using Gross–Pitaevskii–Poisson-based models, I have explored how vortex–flux-tube interactions can produce sudden changes in stellar rotation. My ongoing work extends these ideas to magnetars, investigating their magnetic-field evolution and possible gravitational-wave signatures.

Self-gravitating condensates and dark matter

Ultralight bosonic dark matter provides an intriguing connection between quantum mechanics and cosmology. In these models, dark matter can be described as a large-scale self-gravitating Bose–Einstein condensate, whose dynamics are governed by the interplay between quantum pressure, gravity, and nonlinear interactions.

My research investigates the dynamics of these self-gravitating condensates using the Gross–Pitaevskii–Poisson framework. Through large-scale three-dimensional numerical simulations, I have studied gravitational collapse, phase transitions, the effects of temperature, and the emergence of turbulence and large-scale structures in bosonic and axionic condensates.

Research topics

  • Self-gravitating Bose–Einstein condensates
  • Gravitational collapse and phase transitions
  • Turbulence and large-scale structures
  • Ultralight bosonic and axionic dark matter

How I work

My research combines theoretical modelling, numerical simulations, and statistical analysis to investigate nonlinear fluid phenomena.

I develop and implement numerical models for different physical systems, using methods ranging from spectral simulations of quantum fluids to direct numerical simulations of classical multiphase flows. These simulations are performed using parallel computing techniques, including MPI and OpenMP, and run on high-performance computing clusters.

I am particularly interested in connecting the microscopic dynamics of individual structures, such as vortices, droplets, and interfaces, to the macroscopic statistical behaviour of complex fluid systems.
