About me

I’m a physicist who studies how fluids swirl and break apart, from vortices a nanometre wide in superfluid helium to the insides of neutron stars.

I’m currently a postdoc in the Department of Applied Physics and Science Education at Eindhoven University of Technology, where I work with Federico Toschi on turbulence in complex fluids: droplets, emulsions, and fluids with odd viscosity.

Before coming to Eindhoven, I did my PhD at the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore. My thesis was about superfluids: quantum turbulence in liquid helium, superfluid neutron-star cores and the glitches they cause in pulsars, and self-gravitating condensates that might describe dark matter.

Most of my work comes down to writing down a model, running large simulations of it on supercomputers, and then looking for the statistical patterns in the results.

Quantum turbulence in superfluid helium

Below about 2 K, liquid helium becomes a superfluid that flows without viscosity, and its rotation is carried by quantised vortices. That makes its turbulence a problem spanning many scales at once. I use the two-fluid HVBK model and the Gross–Pitaevskii equation to study its statistics, how small particles get carried around in coflow and counterflow, and how a vibrating nanowire can catch and release a single vortex.

Neutron stars, pulsar glitches and magnetars

Pulsars spin with astonishing regularity, until they suddenly speed up, an event called a “glitch”. Their cores are thought to contain a neutron superfluid and a proton superconductor. With a minimal Gross–Pitaevskii–Poisson model, we showed how vortices and magnetic flux tubes interacting inside the star can produce glitches. I am now extending the model to magnetars, following how their magnetic fields evolve and what gravitational waves they emit.

Related papers
  • A minimal model for pulsar glitches, Phys. Rev. D (2024), covered by New Scientist
  • Dynamics of superfluid–superconducting magnetars, arXiv (2025)

Self-gravitating condensates and dark matter

One idea for dark matter is that it is made of very light bosons that form a Bose–Einstein condensate on galactic scales. I run large 3D simulations of the Gross–Pitaevskii–Poisson equation to see what such a condensate does: how it collapses under gravity, how temperature changes that, and how turbulence and large-scale structures develop inside it.

Related papers

  • Self-gravitating superfluids: the Gross–Pitaevskii–Poisson framework, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A (2026)
  • Vortex retention in bosonic and axionic condensates, Phys. Rev. D (2026)
What I’m working on now

Droplets, emulsions and odd viscosity

In Eindhoven I’ve moved to classical fluids, though the questions are similar. How does a liquid thread pinch off into drops, and what changes if a solid particle is sitting inside it? How do emulsions form in turbulence? And what happens to the turbulent cascade when a fluid has odd viscosity, a kind of viscosity that doesn’t dissipate energy?

Related papers
  • Self-similar breakup of a liquid ligament with a solid particle, arXiv (2026)

How I work. Nearly all of this relies on direct numerical simulations, mostly pseudo-spectral codes parallelised with MPI and OpenMP and run on high-performance computing clusters.
